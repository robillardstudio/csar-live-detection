# Show where languages and machines see differently

**Short answer: the walking field tool best serves people who describe places in more than one language, starting with Yoruba–English bilingual walkers at Ohio State, and the researchers and model builders who have no record of how Yoruba speakers themselves describe a real place. It lets them stand at the same spot, facing the same way, record their own description and the AI's description in Yoruba, English and Spanish, and see on a map where the languages, and the machine's versions of those languages, part ways.** The problem is well documented. The major multilingual image-description benchmarks leave Yoruba out entirely, most "multilingual" AI training data is machine-translated from English, and the one benchmark that includes Yoruba has about 500 items per language and finds small open models scoring near zero on open-ended answers in African languages ([Thapliyal et al.](https://aclanthology.org/2022.emnlp-main.45.pdf); [Aya Vision](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.08751v1.pdf); [Afri-MCQA](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.05699v2)). People describing the same picture in different languages name different things: English, French and Chinese captions together mention 46% more objects than English alone. That has been shown only for stock photos in European and East Asian languages, never for an African language or a real place someone walked through ([Ye et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.14356v5)). The second-best user is the design student learning to observe a site, who is exactly the kind of person attending Ozlem Sulak's embodiment workshop on the Oval. The same paired readings serve both groups, so the workshop is the natural first study. Among the products, research prototypes and Show HN projects reviewed, none combines a person's description and a machine's description of the same spot and facing direction, in several languages, replayable as sight lines; each neighbour covers two of those pieces at most. That makes the professor's non-trivial hypothesis, that the interesting differences lie *between languages* and not only between people and machines, answerable with this tool. It can also be tested before the DH2027 deadline, with the authors as the first walkers. Throughout this report, "not found" means not found in these searches.

## Six possible users, ranked by evidence and fit

The ranking uses five tests. How well documented is the group's pain point? What would the tool let them do that existing tools cannot? How well does the group fit the team: an artist-designer who speaks Yoruba and prefers building tools, and a professor with an art and climate practice who wants between-language hypotheses? Can the work start without outside partners, and how heavy is the ethical load? Which of the upcoming venues does it suit? Earlier rounds of this research recommended comparing "two ledgers" of a walk, the machine's and the walker's, and asking how much of an AI's description of the Oval comes from actually looking. The professor's point that "human versus AI descriptions differ" is a trivial hypothesis is what moves the language comparison to the top. Comparing two ledgers stays obvious until there are ledgers in several languages.

### 1. Bilingual walkers, and the people who need what they say (Yoruba first)

**Who exactly.** The first users are Yoruba–English bilinguals in the OSU and Columbus community, starting with Taiwo, plus Spanish–English bilinguals as a comparison from a well-resourced language. The second circle is the people who would use what the walkers produce: African-language AI builders and evaluators (the Masakhane, AfriAya and AfriCaption communities), linguists studying Yoruba spatial language, and possibly Yoruba teachers and heritage learners. **The documented pain point sits mostly on the data side.** Crossmodal-3600, the standard set of geographically diverse images captioned in 36 languages, does not include Yoruba. Swahili is its only sub-Saharan language ([Thapliyal et al., EMNLP 2022](https://aclanthology.org/2022.emnlp-main.45.pdf)). CVQA's 31 languages include Igbo, Amharic, Oromo, Swahili and Kinyarwanda but not Yoruba ([Romero et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.05967v2)), and Cohere's Aya Vision covers 23 languages with no sub-Saharan African language at all ([Aya Vision report](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.08751v1.pdf)). The training data behind "multilingual" vision models is itself translated. Aya Vision machine-translated rewritten English captions and named "translationese" as a risk; its example is "French press" turned into "French media" ([Aya Vision report](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.08751v1.pdf)). AfriCaption built captions for 20 African languages by translating Flickr8k, a set of stock photos that are not African-sourced ([AfriCaption](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.17405v1)). Afri-MCQA (January 2026) is the one benchmark found that includes Yoruba, with about 500 question-and-answer pairs per language. There, Gemini 2.5 Pro lost under two points from English to Yoruba on multiple-choice questions (82.95% to 81.27%). Small open models lost 15 points, and on open-ended answers, the format closest to "describe this place", small models scored **near zero in native-language queries** ([Afri-MCQA](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.05699v2)). Yoruba writing also has a specific failure mode: earlier tests found ChatGPT "does not always include diacritics", the tone marks that change a Yoruba word's meaning ([Ojo & Ogueji 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.06530)). Model builders say what they lack. AfriAya's creators state that most vision models see Africa "through a Western lens" and misidentify local foods, clothing and settings ([Slator](https://slator.com/cohere-labs-launches-vision-language-dataset-for-african-languages/)). Native captions beat translated ones even in a well-resourced language: in German retrieval, native captions scored 38.4 against 33.4 for machine-translated English ([Buettner & Kovashka, EMNLP 2024](https://par.nsf.gov//servlets/purl/10556936)).

Yoruba itself makes the tool's compass meaningful. A corpus study of over a million words finds that Yoruba builds its spatial terms from **body-part words** across 13 body organs ([JWAL 48(1), 2021](https://journalofwestafricanlanguages.org/downloads/download/140-volume-48-number-1/753-embodied-conceptualisation-in-yoru-ba-evidence-from-spatial-topology)). East and west are sun-based: *ìlà-oòrùn* is "the rising of the sun" and *ìwọ̀-oòrùn* its setting ([Wiktionary mirror](https://kaweah.freedombox.rocks/kiwix/wiktionary_en_all_maxi_2023-04/ila-oorun)). A tool that logs heading with every reading can check whether a speaker's "toward sunrise" matches the way the phone was actually facing. No other tool found can do that. **What the tool would let them do that existing tools cannot.** Camera language apps give single words for objects (LingoLens lists Russian only, no Yoruba) ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/ml/app/lingolens-learn-languages/id6502578008)). Google Translate's camera translates *text* in the scene, not the scene itself ([Google blog](https://blog.google/products/translate/google-translates-instant-camera-translation-gets-upgrade/)). Google Lookout offers 35 languages, none of them Yoruba, and gives detailed descriptions only in English ([Lookout help](https://support.google.com/accessibility/android/answer/9031274?hl=en)). Even Crossmodal-3600's "native" captions followed a style anchored to English COCO captions ([Thapliyal et al.](https://aclanthology.org/2022.emnlp-main.45.pdf)). The field tool would let a Yoruba speaker describe a real place in their own way, at a logged spot and heading, next to the machine's Yoruba and English for the same view. The result is a small, native, open-ended and located set of descriptions, which is exactly the kind of data this literature says is missing.

**Hacker News and YC signals.** The signals here are absences. No HN thread was found about vision models describing scenes in Yoruba or any African language. The YC-backed language startups found (Speak, Univerbal, Pingo, ISSEN) are conversation tutors, and none uses the learner's surroundings ([YC: Speak](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/speak); [YC: Univerbal](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/univerbal); [YC: ISSEN](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/issen)). Blind HN users list "better non-English support" among their wishes for Be My AI ([HN 37673300](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=37673300)). "Describe your own walk in Yoruba, tied to where you stood" is open ground, though the evidence for that is thin. **Risks and ethics.** The main risk is extraction. The models to follow are NaijaVoices' "data farming", in which local facilitators with community standing recruited 1,768 Yoruba voice donors and data was released under CC BY-NC-SA with anonymised IDs ([Emezue et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.20564v3)), and Te Hiku Media's Kaitiakitanga licence, which keeps data sovereignty with the community ([ITU](https://www.itu.int/hub/2022/08/ai-indigenous-languages-maori-te-reo/)). A second risk is essentialism. Findings should be presented as individual walks, never as "how Yoruba people see", and one speaker cannot stand for a language. A third risk is in the analysis: translating Yoruba for comparison with an AI reintroduces exactly the weakness being studied, so a bilingual person has to check every Yoruba translation. Finally, there is **no direct evidence about what Yoruba speakers in Ohio want** from such a tool, and the size of the local community was not found. **Venue fit.** This framing fits DH2027 best. Its review gives 15% to diversity and tells reviewers to reward "non-Western-centric contexts" ([ADHO](https://adho.org/?p=2986); [DH2027 CFP](https://dh2027.adho.org/cfp/)). It also fits LearnXDesign 2027's "Crossings of Perspectives" track ("how ways of knowing interact", "challenging knowledge hierarchies") and its open track's decolonial and pluriversal themes ([LxD 2027](https://www.learnxdesign2027.com/)), a CHI 2027 poster as an instrument-plus-dataset contribution, and later a SIGGRAPH Art Paper presenting the map of differences as the work.

### 2. Design students learning to observe a site

**Who exactly.** Students in design, architecture and landscape architecture who walk sites as part of studio work; the educators who teach site analysis and AI literacy; and, first of all, the participants in the Oval embodiment workshop. **The documented pain point.** Melsom, Fraguada and Hurkxkens argue that the site visit "has remained largely an act of observation and recording", with little analysis done on location ([Melsom et al. 2017](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/158949)). Michigan's landscape students borrowed SiteMarker, a construction documentation app, to drop geotagged pins, citing more accurate observations and less guesswork about where they were standing. The tool has no AI layer ([UMich SEAS](https://seas.umich.edu/news/sitemarker-preparing-future-landscape-architects-advanced-field-documentation-tools)). Designers face "a large conceptual barrier" to understanding AI. In exercises with 100 Master's design students in 28 teams, nobody chose to work directly with datasets ([Murray-Rust et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.01282)). UCL's "Ways of Machine Seeing" set out to teach machine vision to art and design teachers without advanced mathematics or programming ([UCL IOE](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/ioe/departments-and-centres/departments/culture-communication-and-media/ways-machine-seeing-learning-experiments-computer-vision)). The creativity support tool (CST) literature, which inspired the student, shows a gap here. Of 143 CSTs reviewed, **92% ran on desktops or tablets** ([Frich et al. via Pomona slides](https://cs.pomona.edu/classes/cs181dt/assets/slides/L10-CSTs.pdf)). Of 173 recent empirical CST studies, **only 15% measured benefits to the user**, such as developing abilities or self-reflection; about 90% measured usability or experience ([Cox et al., C&C '25](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.01601v1)). A tool used while walking that aims to train noticing sits in both gaps.

**What only this tool does.** Nakakoji's three metaphors for creativity tools are useful here: dumbbells train a skill you keep without the tool, running shoes improve performance while you wear them, and skis enable experiences otherwise impossible ([Pomona slides](https://cs.pomona.edu/classes/cs181dt/assets/slides/L10-CSTs.pdf)). The field tool can be a dumbbell. A student describes what they notice first, then sees what the machine said from the same spot facing the same way, and the disagreements become prompts to look again. The closest precedent is Jane L. E's Stanford work on capture-time camera overlays, which helped novice photographers "train their eye to see in the way that experts do" ([Stanford PURL](https://purl.stanford.edu/pf447rk1452)). SiteMarker and Survey123 record where you were but say nothing. Survey123's compass is a manual form field ([Esri Community](https://community.esri.com/t5/arcgis-survey123-blog/survey123-tricks-of-the-trade-a-built-in-compass/m-p/1340647)). **HN/YC signals.** YC-backed Feldy reads contractors' narrated walkthroughs with vision models to produce estimates faster ([YC Launch: Feldy](https://www.ycombinator.com/launches/SiZ-feldy-field-first-ai-for-home-services-and-contractors/)). A design tool can turn that around and use the walkthrough to slow people down and make them notice. The November 2026 Show HN "Point your camera at a landscape to learn about its geology" drew 256 points and enthusiasm for learning about a place on the spot, but commenters' first question was whether it was "making stuff up" ([HN 50290533](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50290533)). Students need to see where each machine reading came from. The critique Auglinn received, "focus on one use case first", applies here too ([HN 41368277](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=41368277)). **Risks.** Students may not feel free to decline a study run by their own instructors. The novelty of the tool can inflate early results. If the AI speaks first, it primes what students notice, so students should describe before they see the AI. Publishing course data needs IRB review. **Venue fit.** This framing fits LearnXDesign 2027 best. Its "Crossings of Responsibilities" track asks about "accountability when agency is shared with technologies", and "Crossings of Controversies" treats disagreement as a site for learning ([LxD 2027](https://www.learnxdesign2027.com/)). A 3,000–4,000-word Case Study is the realistic format ([LxD submission](https://www.learnxdesign2027.com/submission)). It would also make a CHI poster as a CST.

### 3. Critical-AI researchers auditing how models read real places

**Who.** HCI, critical-AI and urban researchers who study how AI describes public space, and the African-AI groups who complain of the "Western lens". **Pain point.** Their comparisons are made at a desk, and they show poor agreement. In Helsinki, a model's ratings of Street View images matched residents' map pins only **27–29% of the time** under strict matching ([Malekzadeh et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.05570)). In Montréal, residents linked inclusivity with accessibility while the model linked it with aesthetics ([Mushkani & Koseki](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.11708)). Across 3,000 street scenes, model narrations erased **81.7% of the differences between human groups** ([Zhao et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.20048)). The 2024 "AI in the street" observatories walked five cities without any machine vision and named a "reciprocity deficit" between AI infrastructure and the public ([Taylor, Marres et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.23342)). In end-user auditing, side-by-side comparison drove about 74% of reports ([WeAudit](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.01397v1)). **What only this tool does.** It runs the audit on site and by facing direction, with several models and several languages on the same frame, and records which model and settings produced each reading. **HN signals.** "Watching o3 guess a photo's location is surreal, dystopian and entertaining" ([HN 43803243](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=43803243)) and "Vision language models are blind" ([HN 40926734](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=40926734)) show broad interest; neither thread was read in full. **Risks.** A log of where a camera looked and when is structurally a surveillance log. Describing models volunteer age, gender and mood, and large-scale tests found their worst outputs fell most often on Black and obese subjects ([Howard et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.20152)). So the tool should describe places, not people. **Venue fit.** CHI or DIS, and DH. This group is best treated as the *methods audience* for the results of framing 1, not as a separate project.

### 4. Walking-interview and go-along researchers

**Who.** Qualitative researchers, geographers and health and urban researchers who interview people while walking. **Pain point.** The method is mature and active: Carpiano's go-along, Evans and Jones, a 2023 review in the *International Journal of Qualitative Methods* ([Carpiano 2009](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1353829208000622); [Evans & Jones](https://research.manchester.ac.uk/en/publications/exploring-space-and-place-with-walking-interviews/); [Bartlett et al. 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/16094069231164606)). Its tools are not. Researchers usually assemble DIY kits of a GPS logger and a recorder, and these searches found no dominant walking-interview app. The Surrey CAQDAS project documented that linking interview text to places in ATLAS.ti, MAXQDA and NVivo worked one way only: you could jump from a quote to a place but not start from a place and find everything said there. It also warned that combining voices with precise locations "creates the possibility of accidentally revealing the identity of a respondent" ([Surrey CAQDAS](https://www.surrey.ac.uk/computer-assisted-qualitative-data-analysis/archive/integrating-geographical-data)). That evidence dates from about 2010–2012. Current software was not checked. **What only this tool does.** It pins each remark to a position *and* a facing direction, lets a researcher start from a spot or a direction and retrieve everything said there, and allows a "machine go-along", in which the AI's reading becomes the interview prompt ("it says a wide lawn with people; what is it missing?"). No published walking interview using a machine's live reading as the prompt was found. **HN signals.** Requests for GIS and KML export ([HN 41368277](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=41368277)). **Risks.** Re-identification, consent for voice recordings, and IRB review. **Venue fit.** DH and CHI late-breaking work, and a qualitative-methods journal later.

### 5. Artists and walking practitioners

**Who.** Artists who use walking as method, including Taiwo working autobiographically. **Pain point.** This is the most lightly documented group. The ISEA 2025 paper "Walking with AI" paired walkers with *NedeR*, a voice of the Dutch landscape that had no camera, and claimed "enhanced noticing" without evaluation ([ISEA archives](https://www.isea-symposium-archives.org/?p=83553)). The art catalogue splits into works that see without walking (Poetry Camera, Paragraphica) and works that walk without seeing ([Raspberry Pi News](https://www.raspberrypi.com/news/this-camera-writes-poems/); [Paragraphica](https://bjoernkarmann.dk/project/paragraphica)). **What only this tool does.** It provides a machine witness that walks, faces a direction and speaks in several languages. In Nakakoji's terms it is a pair of skis, because it makes possible the experience of hearing your two languages and the machine's versions of them describe the same view. **Risks.** These are low, but the "talking camera" genre is crowded, so the art has to rest on the multilingual comparison, not on the camera talking. **Venue fit.** SIGGRAPH Art Papers, where Gaëtan Robillard's *Critical Climate Machine* (with Jérôme Nika) won Best Art Paper in 2024 ([SIGGRAPH 2024](https://s2024.siggraph.org/program/art-papers/)); ISEA; an LxD Visual Paper.

### 6. Accessibility researchers evaluating descriptions on site

**Who.** Researchers who evaluate AI description for blind and low-vision people. This does not mean blind walkers as end users. **Pain point.** This group has the strongest evidence of any. In a CHI 2025 study of 14 Be My AI users and 50 descriptions, people reported hallucinated objects, static photos that make users "stand there and keep taking pictures", no distance information, and unreliable stair direction ([Xie et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.16098v1)). In an ASSETS 2024 study of 26 blind adults, participants checked AI output **by testing it in familiar places** and by asking sighted people, and reported misidentified culturally specific clothing and poor recognition of non-English text ([Alharbi et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.06546v1)). In a two-week diary study, an AI visual interpreter was wrong **22.2%** of the time and abstained 10.8% of the time ([Gonzalez Penuela et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13469)). HN threads echo this: blind users wanted local models, and commenters stressed that street crossing has "no room for hallucinations" ([HN 45113043](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45113043); [HN 41822433](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=41822433)). **What only this tool does.** It could turn the "familiar place" check into a structured instrument: the same stations, several describers, every reading logged. **Why it ranks last.** The space is crowded and well funded: WorldScribe won Best Paper at UIST 2024, and Google's StreetReaderAI and Apple's SceneScout already exist ([arXiv 2408.06627](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2408.06627); [Google Research](https://research.google/blog/streetreaderai-towards-making-street-view-accessible-via-context-aware-multimodal-ai/); [Apple ML](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/scenescout)). Navigation is safety-critical, free polished incumbents exist, and the work cannot start without paid partners and co-authors. The right stance is "a research instrument, not an assistant". **Venue fit.** ASSETS or CHI, later, with partners.

## Nobody found combines spot, heading, person, machine and language

Every close neighbour of the tool covers two or three of the five pieces that matter. Heading is what makes "the same spot" comparable: two descriptions at one GPS point facing different directions are describing different scenes. Only StreetReaderAI (in virtual Street View) and Mapillary (with no language) treat heading as essential.

The iNaturalist and ODK rows rest on the researchers' prior knowledge and were not re-checked this session. The sharpest way to say what is distinct: **this is the only tool found where a person and a machine describe the same view, from the same spot and facing direction, in more than one language, and the results can be replayed side by side on a map.** Existing tools are built to give one user one description (assistive apps), to put one label on one map (GIS and citizen science), or to caption one image (browser demos). This one compares descriptions.

### What to build next, in order

Each feature below is tied to a research question in the next section, so building it *is* the method.

| Priority | Feature | What it does | Why (evidence) | Serves |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Paired reading, person first** | At a station the walker records their own description (voice or text, in a chosen language), then the AI's reading of the same frame and heading appears | Prevents the machine from priming the person; turns "two ledgers" into data you can compare | RQ1–3; workshop |
| 2 | **Same frame, three languages, plus a "translation twin"** | One frame is described directly in Yoruba, English and Spanish. The AI's English is also machine-translated into Yoruba and Spanish for comparison | The direct test of whether the AI's Yoruba is a translation of its English ([Aya Vision](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.08751v1.pdf)) | RQ1 |
| 3 | **Provenance on every reading** | Model name and version, prompt, language, time, GPS accuracy, heading and heading accuracy | HN users' first question is "is it making stuff up?" ([HN 50290533](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50290533)); models drift and are retired | All |
| 4 | **Places, not people** | The prompt forbids describing people; faces are blurred on the device before any frame leaves the phone; no images of bystanders are stored | Biased descriptions of people ([Howard et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.20152)); smart-glasses doxxing ([HN 41726385](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=41726385)) | Ethics |
| 5 | **Heading calibration and accuracy log** | A calibration step before each walk, heading accuracy saved per reading, and correction after the walk | Phone compasses are unreliable ([Mapillary iOS bug](https://forum.mapillary.com/t/ios-heading-bug/10362)); Survey123 notes magnetic north needs correcting ([Esri](https://community.esri.com/t5/arcgis-survey123-blog/survey123-tricks-of-the-trade-a-built-in-compass/m-p/1340647)) | RQ3 (direction terms) |
| 6 | **Model switch: small on-device vs large cloud** | The same frame is read by a small browser model and a large cloud model | Small models degrade far more in African languages ([Afri-MCQA](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.05699v2)); browser models now run locally ([SmolVLM tutorial](https://pyimagesearch.com/2025/10/20/running-smolvlm-locally-in-your-browser-with-transformers-js/)) | RQ2 |
| 7 | **Tone-mark check** | Flags Yoruba output with missing or wrong tone marks for a bilingual reviewer to confirm | Diacritic omission in AI Yoruba ([Ojo & Ogueji](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.06530); [LREC 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.lrec-1.40/)) | RQ2 |
| 8 | **Sight-wedge replay, coloured by language** | Each reading is drawn as a fan from the walker's position along the heading, carrying the text; a difference view shows what only one language named | Makes the differences visible, which the professor asked for | All; art |
| 9 | **Export to GeoJSON/CSV with heading** | Loads into GIS, spreadsheets and qualitative-coding software | Repeated HN requests for GIS/KML export ([HN 41368277](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=41368277)); Esri users ask for photo direction ([Esri idea](https://community.esri.com/t5/arcgis-survey123-ideas/image-location-amp-direction-on-map-in-report/idi-p/1692676)) | Framings 3–4 |
| 10 | **Describe on demand, not continuous narration** | Hold to describe, with fixed stations to return to | HN feedback that constant narration is unwanted and repetition is tiresome ([HN 42593919](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=42593919)) | Workshop |

Two technical cautions belong in the plan. First, small browser models are mostly English-only, and the one small Yoruba-capable captioner found, AfriCaption (about 0.5B parameters), was trained on translated captions and still makes errors ([AfriCaption](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.17405v1)). Whether iPad Safari runs these models well enough was not tested. Second, the team's own earlier walks found a phone accurate to about ±3 m but a Wi-Fi-only iPad off by about ±17–24 m, so station walks should use a phone or a cellular iPad.

## Three questions, with hypotheses written down before building

The professor asked for a hypothesis stated *before* further development, with tool development as the method. Design research has an established way to do this: Sandoval's **conjecture mapping**. It links the design's features ("embodiment") to the things you expect people to do with it ("mediating processes") and then to outcomes. It separates *design conjectures* ("if the tool does X, people will do Y") from *theoretical conjectures* ("if Y happens, Z follows") ([Sandoval 2014](https://draft.epsy5221.education.uconn.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2358/2021/08/Sandoval-2014-Conjecture-Mapping-An-Approach-to-Systematic-Educational-Design-Research.pdf)). Gaver warns that design-research theory is "provisional, contingent and aspirational" ([Gaver 2012](https://research.gold.ac.uk/14818)), so the hypotheses below should be written as conjectures the team will revise, and given a date stamp before the next features are built. Putting them in the DH2027 submission, or in a dated registration, does that. All three questions concern differences between languages, as the professor asked, and each predicts a direction that could turn out wrong.

| Research question | Hypothesis, stated in advance | What it rests on | What would count against it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **RQ1. Is the AI's Yoruba its own way of seeing, or its English in translation?** | **H1: The AI's Yoruba will be a translation of its English.** For the same frame, the AI's Yoruba description will share more of its named things and relations with its own English description than a Yoruba speaker's description shares with an English speaker's (or with the same bilingual person's English) for the same view. It will also sit closer to a machine translation of its English than to any human Yoruba description. | Translated training pipelines ([Aya Vision](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.08751v1.pdf); [AfriCaption](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.17405v1)); a classifier tells English-captioned images from non-English ones at 67%, and translated captions still differ in content ([Nguyen et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.16915v3)); the "English accent" of LLM output ([arXiv 2410.15956](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.15956v3)) | The AI's Yoruba–English overlap is the same as, or lower than, the human Yoruba–English overlap |
| **RQ2. Is the gap between people and the AI the same size in every language?** | **H2: The human–AI gap will be larger in Yoruba than in English or Spanish.** The share of things speakers mention that the AI's same-language description misses will be largest in Yoruba. Tone-mark errors will appear only in Yoruba and will be more frequent for the small on-device model than the large cloud model. | Near-zero open-ended scores for small models in African languages, but a gap of under two points for Gemini 2.5 Pro on multiple choice ([Afri-MCQA](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.05699v2)); diacritic errors ([Ojo & Ogueji](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.06530)); Spanish is in every major benchmark, Yoruba in almost none | Gaps are similar across the three languages, or the gap disappears for the large model (a finding in itself: the gap is about model size, not language) |
| **RQ3. Do people vary more across languages than the machine does?** | **H3: Human descriptions will vary more across languages than AI descriptions do.** At each station, the spread among human descriptions in Yoruba, English and Spanish will exceed the spread among the AI's three descriptions. A sharper prediction specific to Yoruba: speakers will use more relational, body-based spatial terms and direction words than the AI's Yoruba does, and their sun-based direction terms can be checked against the logged heading. | Multilingual human captions add +46.0% objects and +66.1% relations over English alone, but model captions add only about +22%; Google's model varied least, which the authors attributed to "more standardised captions" ([Ye et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.14356v5)); English captions mention objects about 1.5× as often as German ([Buettner & Kovashka](https://par.nsf.gov//servlets/purl/10556936)); Yoruba body-part spatial terms ([JWAL 2021](https://journalofwestafricanlanguages.org/downloads/download/140-volume-48-number-1/753-embodied-conceptualisation-in-yoru-ba-evidence-from-spatial-topology)) | The AI's cross-language spread equals or exceeds the human spread; Yoruba speakers use no more spatial or direction terms than the AI |

One design choice makes RQ1 unusually clean, and it is available because of who Taiwo is. A bilingual person describing the same view in Yoruba and in English holds the describer constant, just as the AI does. The pilot can therefore compare **one bilingual person across two languages with one "bilingual" machine across two languages**. If the person's two descriptions differ more than the machine's two, the difference comes from the languages, not from differences between people. A speaker's Yoruba and English descriptions should come from separate walks, in alternating order, so the first description does not shape the second.

### How building the tool answers each question

| Question | What to build | Small study | What to measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| RQ1 | Features 1, 2, 3 (paired reading, three languages plus translation twin, provenance) | Taiwo walks 10–12 fixed stations, each with a fixed heading, once in Yoruba and once in English. The AI reads each frame in Yoruba, English and Spanish. Later, 4–6 speakers per language | Overlap of named things and relations after translation into English (Ye et al.'s method, whose GPT-4 translations were rated 4.68/5), with **a bilingual human checking every Yoruba translation** |
| RQ2 | Features 6, 7 (model switch, tone-mark check) | Same stations, read by a small on-device model and a large cloud model | Share of human-mentioned items missing from the AI in the same language; tone-mark error rate per 100 words, confirmed by a bilingual reader |

The **conjecture map** fits on one page. *High-level conjecture:* placing people's and the machine's descriptions in several languages side by side, at the same spot and facing direction, will show that the machine's many languages behave like one language in translation, while people's languages notice different things. *Embodiment:* features 1–8. *Mediating processes (observable):* walkers describe first, then compare; they mark what only their language named; they correct the AI's Yoruba. *Outcomes:* the three measures above, walkers' reflections, a small located dataset in three languages, and a set of difference maps. Each arrow in the map names the data that would show it, which is how the map keeps "tool development as method" honest.

**Evaluation sized for a small project.** Shneiderman recommends in-depth, repeated case studies over benchmark tests for creative tools ([Shneiderman 2007](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~ben/papers/Shneiderman2007Creativity.pdf)). A DIS 2026 design probe with 15 walking participants, interviews and thematic analysis was accepted at a top venue ([UnReality Camera](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.02805v1)). Ledo et al. accept demonstration as a legitimate evaluation for tool papers ([Ledo et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1145/3173574.3173610)). A defensible plan has three steps. First, an author pilot (autobiographical design) before 20 November. Second, the workshop as the first study with other people. Third, 4–6 speakers per language at the same stations in early 2027. Results should be reported as descriptive comparisons with worked examples and maps, coded by two people, one of them a Yoruba speaker, with no claims of statistical significance. That supports claims about *where and how* languages differ, not *how much in general*. For the design-education strand at LearnXDesign, one extra conjecture tests the dumbbell idea: after walking with paired readings, students will record more kinds of observation on a later walk *without* the tool, mostly at stations where they and the AI disagreed. The CST field measures this kind of outcome least often ([Cox et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.01601v1)).

## A closing paragraph for the literature review

This draft is about 195 words and is written to close the literature review the professor requested:

> Across these literatures, three findings converge. Creativity support tools have been built almost entirely for desks and tablets and evaluated mostly for usability, rarely for what their users learn to notice (Frich et al. 2019; Cox et al. 2025). Vision-language models now describe scenes in many languages, but much of that ability is built by translating English, Yoruba is absent from the main image-description benchmarks, and the evidence that people describing the same image in different languages notice different things comes only from stock photographs in European and East Asian languages (Thapliyal et al. 2022; Buettner and Kovashka 2024; Ye et al. 2025). Walking methods study place through the body, yet their walkers carry no machine that sees, and machine and human readings of a place are compared at a desk (Taylor et al. 2025; Breken et al. 2025; Malekzadeh et al. 2025). Missing is a way to set a person's and a machine's descriptions of the same place side by side, from the same spot and facing the same way, across languages, Yoruba included. This project treats tool development as its method: each research question is answered by extending the field tool and walking the Oval with it.

## What these searches did not verify

Some claims could not be confirmed. **Yoruba speakers' own needs** are the largest gap: no study of what Yoruba heritage learners, diaspora families or teachers want from camera or AI tools was found, and the size of the Columbus Yoruba-speaking community is unknown. The beneficiaries named on the data side are well evidenced; those on the speaker side are inferred. Peer-reviewed work on Yoruba frames of reference (how speakers choose between body-based, relative and absolute directions) appears thin. The JWAL article's authorship is uncertain, the cardinal-direction glosses come from a Wiktionary mirror, and the proposed Yoruba place categories (market, compound, crossroads) are unverified. That is why the hypothesis that the AI will frame a US campus in Western categories was left out of the core three. Sources conflict on two points. One note reports that AfriCaption includes Yoruba among its best-scoring languages, while the abstract does not confirm this. CVQA's figures differ between versions of the paper; this report uses version 2. Ye et al.'s headline percentages also differ between versions, and version 5 is quoted here. Afri-MCQA did not measure whether models answer in English when asked in Yoruba. The NeurIPS 2026 paper "The Multilingual Artificial Hivemind" was seen by title only and could partly pre-empt RQ3 ([NeurIPS](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/164689)). Read it before submitting.

On the landscape, details of iNaturalist, ODK, KoBoToolbox, QField, Fulcrum, Aira, ATLAS.ti geo-documents and Felt came from prior knowledge and were not re-checked. Participatory-GIS tools such as Maptionnaire, and soundwalk or locative-art apps, were not checked. The ACM Digital Library was not searched directly for a prototype combining on-site AI description, heading and human annotation. So "nobody offers this combination" means nobody *found*. Only 8 Hacker News threads were read in full because of rate limits, and the language-app threads (Snapalabra, Lingosnap) and the large privacy threads were not. The Frich et al. definition wording, Remy et al.'s findings and Nakakoji's metaphors (taken from lecture slides) were not checked against the original papers. The CHI 2027 poster deadline was not verified. Whether a Master's or MFA student is eligible for LearnXDesign's PhD Crossings format was not checked. Whether the "Open Art" conference that inspired the student was an OSU event could not be confirmed. Whether the small browser models run well on iPad Safari, and how well any model describes outdoor scenes in Yoruba, can only be settled by testing on site. Finally, the tool's earlier object detector placed people on the map by assuming every person is 1.7 m tall. That assumption distorts distances for children, seated people and wheelchair users. It is a known limitation of the detector's distance estimates and should be stated as one. It is not a research direction, and it does not affect the describing models.

## Conclusion

The question "who does this serve?" has a less obvious answer than it first seemed. The tool's user is not someone who needs help seeing, a market that free, polished assistive apps already hold. The user is someone who needs to *compare* ways of seeing and has no instrument for it. Bilingual walkers are the clearest case, because they carry the comparison inside themselves. Taiwo walking the Oval once in Yoruba and once in English, next to a machine doing the same, is a better first experiment than any recruited panel. It holds the describer constant and lets the languages show their differences. It also answers the HN critique that every place-notes app hears, "why would anyone want this?", with a named person and a named task.

The deeper implication concerns what the machine's multilingualism is. If H1 holds, a describing AI's many languages are one language with many vocabularies, and only people standing in a place can show what its translations leave out. That turns low-resource languages from a gap in a benchmark table into the place where the most can be learned. It also gives the project a stance toward community data that is ethical and productive at once: Yoruba speakers are the only ones who can supply what the machine lacks, so the work has to be built with them, on their terms, from the first walk.
